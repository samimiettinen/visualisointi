# Choosing and composing visual forms

Choose the relationship the audience needs to inspect before choosing a page template. These are starting constructions, not a quota of shapes or a fixed style. If a visual needs information the source does not provide, simplify it or identify the missing evidence.

## Forms and the evidence they require

| Audience question | Useful form | Construction | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What changed between two states? | Dumbbell, slope, paired panels | Common scale, matched categories, direct labels | A hidden intervening trend; unmatched cases |
| Where do patterns differ? | Small multiples | Repeated form, shared scales, logical panel order | Each panel autoscaling and exaggerating differences |
| What contributes to the result? | Waterfall or aligned decomposition | A reconciled start, contributions and end | Overlapping contributions or an unexplained residual |
| What differs between options? | Comparison matrix or aligned profiles | Same criteria in the same order; relevant evidence beside each | Arbitrary scores and unequal evidence |
| What happens, and who handles it? | Swimlane or process diagram | Actors, steps, labelled hand-offs, exceptions | Decorative arrows implying unverified causality |
| How does the system work? | Layered architecture or mechanism diagram | Named components, boundary, interfaces and direction | Boxes of nouns with no explained relationships |
| How does the object work? | Annotated image, cutaway or exploded view | Authentic object or labelled schematic, local callouts | A decorative illustration presented as real anatomy |
| Where does it happen? | Map with inset or aligned local detail | Meaningful geography, source, scale appropriate to the task | Mapping ranked values when location adds nothing |
| How uncertain is the estimate? | Interval, range, fan or scenario plot | Defined bounds and visibly distinct scenarios | An unexplained confidence band or false precision |
| How are cases distributed? | Dot plot, histogram or box plot | Units, sample, suitable bins and exceptions | Replacing the spread with an average alone |
| How do parts connect? | Network, hierarchy or flow | Declared node and edge meanings; focus path | An impressive hairball without an answer |
| How does a whole divide? | Stacked bar, treemap or proportional flow | Common denominator and mutually exclusive parts | Unmeasured areas or widths that look quantitative |
| What is the real experience? | Annotated screenshot or photo sequence | Actual evidence, meaningful crops, stable sequence | Fake interface details or repeated stock filler |
| What must a reader decide? | Structured analytical page | Decision, comparable evidence, constraints and next step | A wall of text or sparse slogans replacing analysis |

Use a qualitative diagram when only relationships are known. Do not assign quantitative widths, areas, coordinates or scores without data. Mark schematic geometry when the distinction matters.

## Compositions beyond chart-left / text-right

**Evidence plate.** Give a diagram, map or chart most of the page. Place short annotations beside the specific feature they explain. Keep a compact title and source. Good for technical explanations and analytical findings.

**Aligned comparison.** Arrange two or more cases on common axes or a shared grid. Put identical labels in identical positions. Use text to explain exceptions, not to make the audience reconstruct the comparison.

**Overview plus detail.** Show the whole system and a larger inset of the relevant part. Connect the inset unambiguously. Preserve orientation, units and scale disclosure; do not imply equal scale where it differs.

**Layered analytical page.** Place the decision or finding first; group a few related evidence blocks beneath it; use a smaller reference layer for definitions and sources. A primary chart, supporting comparison and caveat can serve one purpose together.

**Image with interpretation.** Let an authentic image establish the context. Crop with intent, preserve evidential details and attach local callouts. Use a separate caption for provenance. If no appropriate image exists, choose a diagram or text without a decorative placeholder.

**Sequence.** Show states, time or hand-offs in the order that matters. Align recurring objects so the change can be seen. Use animation only if it helps the audience track that change; the static export must remain understandable.

**Stable context with changing focus.** Repeat the same system while emphasizing one justified route or component at a time. Hold positions, scale and meanings constant. Use a text label, badge or line treatment as well as colour. Keep other parts readable and include the whole explanation in the static delivery. Atlas pages 7–8 demonstrate this construction.

## Depth comes from relationships

A good visual can have several reading levels: the main pattern is apparent quickly, while labels and annotations support closer inspection. Arrange detail around the same question. Density is useful when it reveals variation or permits comparison; density is harmful when it forces unrelated material together.

For a page that feels too simple, try the first relevant improvement:

- Replace verbal comparison with spatial alignment or a shared axis.
- Show variation, outliers, the denominator or an uncertainty interval the summary omitted.
- Explain a mechanism through connections and boundaries.
- Add an overview or detail inset that resolves an actual ambiguity.
- Use a real object, document or image as evidence and annotate its important part.
- Add a materially different case or counterexample when the source supports it.

Do not add all of these. Do not manufacture complexity for visual novelty.

## Labels and connections

Use position and proximity before enclosing every item in a box. Make grouping visible with spacing, alignment, small backgrounds or rules. Match connector endpoints to the objects they actually connect. Label ambiguous arrows with a verb or transmitted quantity. Keep connectors out of text and distinguish feedback, conditional routes and ordinary flow.

Colour can reinforce roles, but the reader should also have words, positions or symbols. Direct labels often reduce legend lookup. A legend is appropriate when it is clearer than colliding labels. Link a detail to its location with a leader or inset; do not float commentary far from the evidence.
