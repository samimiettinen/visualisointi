# Story and reading conditions

Start with what the audience needs to understand or decide. A persuasive deck may have a governing argument; a technical lesson may have a sequence of questions; a reference deck may be organised for lookup. Preserve the user's intended order where specified.

## Storyboard both meaning and form

For each page record: purpose, supported message, source material, visual form and any unresolved gap. A short table is enough. Titles alone expose narrative gaps, but a coherent list of titles does not prove that the pages contain useful visual explanations.

For substantial new work, use [the design worksheet](../assets/design-worksheet.md). Roughly place evidence and labels before polishing type and colour. Where two forms could explain the same material, sketch their reading paths and choose the one that makes the important relationship visible. Keep this proportionate to the task.

- For an executive read deck, give the decision or finding early, then the evidence and material qualifications.
- For a talk, use progression, contrast, demonstrations and occasional pauses. A meaningful question can establish curiosity without hiding information the audience needs.
- For a comparison, keep the objects, measures and scales stable across related pages.
- For a tutorial, show intermediate states where they help explain the mechanism. Label assumptions and feedback paths.

## Sketch before polishing

For a substantial new deck, place rough evidence blocks on a small canvas before choosing type and colour. A rough SVG, a plain table or a native slide sketch is enough.

Where the form is uncertain, sketch two alternatives with the same facts. For a process with exceptions, compare a straight timeline with an actor-based route diagram: choose the route diagram if responsibility and return paths matter, the timeline if elapsed time is the question and timing evidence exists. Decide on explanatory value before styling, and do not make variants just to have them.

## Reuse a pattern for its purpose

A useful library stores explanations, not attractive empty layouts. Reuse only user-provided, approved or original material, and keep it in the user's workspace; never bring in confidential examples from another client.

| Pattern | Question it answers | Inputs it needs | What must survive adaptation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Comparison plate | Which option differs on the relevant criteria? | Matched cases and definitions | Common ordering, scale and comparable evidence |
| Route diagram | Who handles this case, including exceptions? | Actors, conditions and hand-offs | Labelled paths, boundaries and returns |
| Decision read page | What supports this decision, and what could change it? | Finding, evidence, constraints | Main evidence and material caveats together |
| Object study | How does this part or product work? | Authentic image or declared schematic | Orientation, meaningful features and local labels |
| Context and focus | Which part of this system matters now? | A stable whole and a justified focus | Geometry, readable context and explicit emphasis |
| Valuation page | What is the company worth, and on what basis? | Methods, ranges, basis (EV or equity), reference price | One value basis, labelled reference line, stated periods |

When reusing a pattern, replace every inherited name, figure, date, label, chart range and note. Do not carry over a previous conclusion because it fits the layout. Masters and placeholders help recurring layouts; a complex diagram may need grouped objects instead.

## One deck for a meeting and for later reading

Separate the main route from optional detail in the outline. Give supporting pages descriptive titles and reference them from the main page; where the format supports links, add a return link and test it after export, otherwise use page numbers. The meeting need not visit every supporting page, but a qualification that could overturn the recommendation stays on the main page, not in notes or an appendix.

## Titles

Use a takeaway when the evidence establishes it, a descriptive title when the page explains a subject, and a question when the question is real. Keep useful uncertainty: “could”, “estimated” and a range may be essential. Do not turn association into cause or an estimate into a fact.

| Page job | Suitable title, assuming the source supports it |
| --- | --- |
| Finding | Approval queues account for most of the delay |
| Process explanation | How a purchase request reaches approval |
| Comparison | Approval routes for routine and exceptional requests |
| Open decision | Which exceptions still need manual review? |
| Reference | Definitions and measurement periods |

Avoid repeating a finding in several successive titles. A cover and closing page can echo the central idea when that helps recall. Section labels and agendas are useful navigation when the audience needs them; there is no universal slide-count cutoff.

## Density

Speaker-led pages are viewed while listening. Make primary labels and the message readable at the expected distance; put optional detail in notes. Self-read pages need enough context to stand alone: definitions, sources, caveats and connected evidence can belong on the page.

Word counts are warning signals, not content limits. A readable matrix of 150 words may be easier than 40 words scattered among unrelated icons. Test the rendered page at its intended size before splitting or cutting. A read deck that will also be projected may need a simplified main view and a detailed companion.

Use a clear entry point, grouped supporting material and a distinguishable reference layer. Give a dense page enough explanation to be read correctly. Never reduce key labels below a readable size simply to retain a layout.

Check each layer separately: the quick reader can find the point, the interested reader can inspect the evidence, and the careful reader can find assumptions and sources. A caveat that could change the conclusion belongs near that conclusion. Optional lookup detail can go in a labelled appendix with explicit page references or tested links.

## Rhythm

The sequence should vary in intellectual effort: establish the question, inspect evidence, explain a mechanism, compare alternatives and reach a decision. Visual variation follows these changes. Repeated comparison layouts, identical axes and small multiples are strengths when they reduce the reader's relearning effort.

A closing page can contain an actionable decision, next steps, a useful synthesis or discussion questions. Choose what the occasion requires. Do not invent names or dates to manufacture an ask.

## Preserve material

Keep source facts and required content traceable through restructuring. Consolidate duplication and move supporting detail to notes or an appendix when appropriate; do not silently drop qualifications or competing evidence. If sources disagree, show the difference or flag the unresolved issue.
